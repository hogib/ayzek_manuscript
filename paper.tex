\begin{abstract}
  Early-warning detectors must run continuously on modest hardware, yet learned ones
  usually need a machine-learning runtime and are evaluated on curated windows or
  tuned on their test records. We present ayzek, a lightweight detection pipeline
  whose hand-written networks reproduce the trained models to within rounding, and a
  replay protocol, fixed before any test data were fetched, for measuring detectors at
  their operating point with chance correction, paired tests and catalog-quiet data.
  The pipeline's three 142\,059-parameter networks score a window in 5.2~ms, about 1\%
  of the 0.5~s scoring interval, so one desktop thread serves 95 stations and a
  Raspberry Pi~4 serves 52 with identical alarms. All settings, the baselines'
  included, were chosen on Turkish development data before testing on the Greek
  networks and New Zealand's GeoNet, unseen by the learned detector, and the KOERI
  network, held out in time. At its development alarm level, the learned detector
  detected 150.3 of 177 Greek events after chance correction against 127.9 for a
  short-term average/long-term average (\stalta{}) trigger (difference 22.4, 95\%
  interval 11.7--33.5), though with 371 unmatched alarms against 189, and 84.2 of 151
  New Zealand events against 66.1 (18.1, 7.2--29.5); KOERI's 36 events gave 2.5
  ($-0.5$ to 5.9). Causal PhaseNet was equivalent within $\pm$10\% on both unseen
  networks, so the gain is not specific to our detector. Its catalog-quiet alarm rates
  were not distinguishable from \stalta{}'s (New Zealand 0.9 against 1.6 per day;
  Greece 19.3 against 20.7, half from one noisy station), and its alarms came
  1.2--2.9~s later. Code and protocol are open.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Earthquake early warning (EEW) systems detect an earthquake from the first seconds
of P waves and alert sites before the strong shaking reaches them. Public systems
share a design: a telemetered network, a pipeline that detects and characterizes the
event, and an alert policy. Japan's nationwide system has run since 2007 on
point-source estimates from the stations nearest the hypocenter \citep{hoshiba2008},
joined in 2018 by PLUM, which propagates observed shaking instead of a source
\citep{kodera2020}. Taiwan's system reports an event within 20~s from a strong-motion
network \citep{hsiao2009} densified by low-cost MEMS sensors \citep{wu2013}.
ShakeAlert's EPIC and FinDer were tested on a replay platform before public rollout
\citep{kohler2018,cochran2018,chung2019,kohler2020}; Mexico's SASMEX has alerted for
thirty years \citep{suarez2022}; Israel's TRUAA \citep{nof2021} and Istanbul's
threshold-based system \citep{alcik2009} are regional examples. In each, a detector
runs continuously on every station, and the P travel time to the stations needed to
declare an event bounds the warning the system can give \citep{allen2009}.

In Europe, PRESTo \citep{zollo2009} is a free regional platform, tested on Greek
Ionian stations \citep{bracale2021}, and the Virtual Seismologist and FinDer run as
modules of the SeisComP monitoring framework \citep{behr2016,massin2021,porras2021};
EPIC has been replayed on Turkish data \citep{tunc2024,marcou2025,budakoglu2026}.
\citet{meier2020} ran PLUM, EPIC and FinDer on 219 Japanese earthquakes with common
scoring, the closest precedent to the replay comparisons of this study. Low-cost
designs move processing toward the sensor, from fixed smartphones with a cloud back
end \citep{brooks2021} to PLUM on Raspberry Shake nodes \citep{prasanna2022};
deep-learning workflows such as QuakeFlow \citep{zhu2023} and single-station models
such as CREIME \citep{chakraborty2022} run under Python runtimes, offline or in the
cloud. All of these detect with amplitude or picker triggers, or with learned models
that need a runtime, inside a hosting framework.

Classical triggers such as the ratio of short- to long-term averages
\citep{allen1978,withers1998} and multi-band pickers such as FilterPicker
\citep{lomax2012} are inexpensive and time an onset to the sample, but an amplitude
criterion sensitive enough for small earthquakes also fires on impulsive cultural and
instrumental transients. Deep networks trained to detect earthquakes
\citep{perol2018,ross2018,mousavi2019cred} and to pick phases
\citep{zhu2019,mousavi2020eqt} are mostly evaluated on curated windows with balanced
classes, as the standardized datasets \citep{mousavi2019stead} and model collections
\citep{woollam2022} encourage, although learned pickers do run on continuous data
\citep{mousavi2020eqt,yeck2021} and transfer well between regions at similar
distances \citep{munchmeyer2022}.

Curated windows hide the cost that matters in operation: over hours without
seismicity, a detector's false alarms accumulate. On balanced windows, a metric such
as the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC) says little
about the false-alarm rate at the operating point. ROC-AUC is insensitive to class
prevalence \citep{fawcett2006}, which precision--recall analysis
\citep{davis2006,saito2015} and partial areas at low false positive rates
\citep{mcclish1989} address; in seismic detection the operating region is extreme,
one window false alarm per day among 172\,800 decisions per station and day being a
false positive rate below $10^{-5}$. When a detector is evaluated on continuous
records, it and its baselines are usually tuned on records from the same network and
period. Comparisons of detectors at a fixed alarm budget on continuous records remain
rare, and rarer still with false alarms measured on catalog-quiet data.

In this study we evaluate detectors on continuous recordings at a fixed alarm budget.
We built ayzek, a detection pipeline whose station stage is a learned detector trained
on Turkish data, and chose every setting of it and of four baselines, an \stalta{}
trigger, FilterPicker, PhaseNet and EQTransformer, on development replays. The test
protocol was fixed and timestamped before any test waveform was fetched; the detectors
were then replayed on earthquake sequences and catalog-quiet windows from Greece, New
Zealand and, held out in time, the Turkish KO network.

The computational contribution of this study is twofold. The first is ayzek, a
lightweight, self-contained pipeline from miniSEED decoding to the network
alarm and a first magnitude, with hand-written inference and no machine-learning
runtime, verified against its PyTorch models, deterministic in replay, and running
unchanged on a Raspberry Pi~4. Unlike the systems above, its station stage is a
learned detector in one binary that runs on a single-board computer, without a
hosting framework. The second is a replay protocol,
implemented as a reproducible workflow, that measures detection at the operating
point. Chance matches with the catalog are corrected by shifting the catalog in time,
only the stations that raised an alarm are matched, detectors are compared with
paired tests, and false alarms are counted on data without cataloged seismicity.

The geoscientific contribution is a measurement of how learned and classical
detectors behave on continuous seismic records from three networks under that
protocol: the five Greek networks served by the National Observatory of
Athens and the New Zealand national network, neither used to train the learned
detector, and the
Turkish KO network on which the detector was trained, held out in time. The
pipeline's learned detector is compared with a recursive \stalta{} trigger,
FilterPicker \citep{lomax2012} and the published EQTransformer
\citep{mousavi2020eqt} and PhaseNet \citep{zhu2019} models, to test whether any gain
over the classical trigger is specific to our detector. In this study an alarm is a network detection with a first magnitude rather
than a warning that an operational system could issue, and all results come from
replays of archived data. The study asks whether the advantage of a learned detector
over classical triggers, measured on development data, survives on networks it has
not seen, at equal false-alarm rates.

\section{Material and methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Development and test data}\label{sec:data}

All choices in this study were made on development data; the test results come from
data that none of these choices used, with one exception, the per-station \stalta{}
ratios of Section~\ref{sec:protocol}. The learned detector was trained on 6~s
three-component windows from 181 stations of the KO \citep{koeri1971}, 6G
\citep{koeri2013marsite} and IJ \citep{iu2013} networks of the Kandilli Observatory
and Earthquake Research Institute (KOERI), around cataloged events of $M \geq 2$ up
to 29~August 2026, partitioned by station into training, validation and test sets
(Section~S19). The development
replays (Table~\ref{tab:replays}, Figure~\ref{fig:map-dev}) come from the continuous
archive of broadband stations of the Turkish National Seismic Network
\citep[TU;][]{afad1990}, operated by the Disaster and Emergency Management Authority
(AFAD) in the Marmara and northern Aegean regions and absent from the training set.
With three public KOERI sets, they comprise two tuning windows, two sequences and 23
quiet windows (102.8~h). One KOERI set overlaps the detector's training data and
serves as a demonstration only.
Events are scored only at stations where they are visible, with the signal-to-noise
ratio of the 2--20~Hz vertical component around the IASP91 P arrival $t_P$ (times in
seconds),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:snr}
  \mathrm{SNR} = \mathrm{RMS}\bigl(x[t_P - 1,\ t_P + 12]\bigr)/
  \mathrm{RMS}\bigl(x[t_P - 60,\ t_P - 10]\bigr),
\end{equation}
of at least 3.

A station is used if it records three HH channels at 100~Hz with at least 90\%
coverage of the test period and its
components can be rotated to north and east; coordinates and gains come from each
network's StationXML (Table~\ref{tab:test-networks}).

\paragraph{Sequences.} Each network's test pool holds all events of $M \geq 4.5$ in
its catalog within its test period that have at least five usable stations within
150~km; the catalogs are EMSC's \citep{emsc2026catalogue} for KO, NOA's for Greece
and GeoNet's \citep{geonet1970catalogue} for New Zealand. In time order, each such event
starts a sequence unless it lies within 3~h after the first event of an earlier
sequence with which its stations share at least one; it then belongs to that
sequence. A sequence is replayed for 3~h from 10~min before its first event on its
nearest usable stations within 150~km, at most eight and at least five, and its
cataloged events within 250~km of the stations' centroid are scored as on the
development data. The test periods end at the release date (2026-10-30 12:55:36 UTC).
Each sequence is assigned, in time order, to this study or to a reserve for later
tests by a pseudo-random draw (seed 42). This study's sequences are taken in time
order until their scorable cataloged events, counted from the catalog before any
waveform is fetched, reach the target, 240 on KO and 150 on Greece and New Zealand,
the crossing sequence included whole; a network that does not reach its target uses
all of its sequences and is reported as under the target, as KO's two-month pool was
expected to be.

\paragraph{Quiet windows.} Each network also contributes up to 24 windows of 6~h,
drawn at random over its test period with seed 43 on the station sets of its
sequences, and accepted only if no cataloged event lies within 250~km of any of
their stations during the window or 30~min on either side and their data cover 90\%
of it, small distant events being disregarded if 10\,000 draws give fewer than 24.
The first 30~min of each window are not scored.

\subsection{Detectors}\label{sec:detectors}

The pipeline's own detector, called the learned detector throughout, scores 6~s
windows. Each window ($600 \times 3$ samples at 100~Hz) is detrended, tapered,
band-passed at 1--45~Hz, standardized per component and compressed with an inverse
hyperbolic sine. The filter is zero-phase but applied within the window, whose last
sample is the current time, so that no later sample is used; the standardization uses
the window's own statistics, which removes gain and site amplitude. Three strided convolutional layers reduce
the window to 75 steps of 96 channels, and a bidirectional LSTM with 48 units per
direction, a four-head self-attention block with residual connection
\citep{vaswani2017} and a small perceptron produce a single logit (142\,059
parameters). The arrangement is that of CRED \citep{mousavi2019cred} with the self-attention of
EQTransformer's encoder \citep{mousavi2020eqt}, adapted from 1D2D-EDL
\citep{wang2025}. The network was trained with label smoothing, three seeds are averaged, and the
ensemble reaches a ROC-AUC of 0.9785 on the station-disjoint curated test set. A P/S
picker, run only after a trigger, provides picks for location (Section~S19).

The recursive \stalta{} band-passes the trace with fourth-order Butterworth sections,
forms the squared vertical component or the three-component energy, and agrees with
SciPy \citep{virtanen2020} and ObsPy \citep{beyreuther2010,krischer2015} to within
rounding (Section~S2). FilterPicker takes the maximum over a bank of band-pass characteristic functions
and declares a pick when its mean over a window reaches a second threshold; the
pipeline's port of Lomax's C code gives identical picks (Section~S12). EQTransformer and
PhaseNet run through SeisBench 0.12.6 in place of the learned detector, each 6~s window
receiving the largest model output within it, EQTransformer's detection trace or
PhaseNet's P probability. In the \emph{causal} mode the model input ends at the end
of the window, as in real-time operation; in the \emph{look-ahead} mode it is
centered on it, an offline upper bound (Section~S8). Each baseline replaces only the learned detector's output;
triggers are limited to one per 15~s per station, and everything downstream is
unchanged.

\subsection{Real-time pipeline}\label{sec:pipeline}

The pipeline, written in C++23, runs from ingestion to location and magnitude (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}). Each station is served by an
ingestion and a processing thread that feed one network stage through an ordered
message bus, and the build is one binary and a directory of weights. Records are
released in the arrival order of a live feed, and a ring buffer whose positions give
the sample times neither drops nor blocks writes (Section~S2).

Windows are scored on an absolute grid every 0.5~s. Label smoothing makes the
learned detector's outputs saturate between 0.90 and 0.907, so that a single threshold at
$p \geq 0.9$ depends on the third decimal. The adopted rule, chosen on the tuning
replays (Table~S18), fires when eight
consecutive windows reach $p \geq 0.8$, 3.5~s after the first, or when one window
reaches $p \geq 0.9$; a trigger re-arms after two windows below 0.3, and no window
within 15~s of the previous trigger may start a run. The P time of a learned trigger
is the first sample in its window at which a recursive \stalta{} (2--20~Hz, 1 and
30~s, vertical) running alongside reaches a ratio of 3, otherwise 3.5~s into the
window; this sample always lies in the past, and the anchoring adds no latency. Two
detections are associated when their P times differ by no more than the P travel time
between the stations plus 3~s, an event is declared when two stations agree, and
later detections at a station within 40~s are absorbed as its coda. Location, a
two-stage grid search in a half-space from accepted P and S picks, serves the report
after the alarm and is not evaluated (Section~S16).

All layers are implemented by hand and load the PyTorch \citep{paszke2019}
parameters unchanged; agreement tests on real waveforms put the worst-case error of
the detector logit at $6.0 \times 10^{-7}$, and repeated and concurrent replays
produce byte-identical reports. On a Ryzen~5 5600 a window of the three-model
ensemble costs 5.2~ms against 0.0005~ms for the \stalta{}, and one thread sustains
95 stations; on a Raspberry Pi~4 the static aarch64 build raises the same alarms at
the same times and four cores sustain 52 stations (Table~S4). The tests run the
release's static x86-64 build, whose reports on all development replays equal the
development build's.

\subsection{Magnitude and early location}\label{sec:magnitude}

The magnitude is estimated from the peak displacement of the P wave, Pd
\citep{wu2005,kanamori2005}, rather than learned. At each station with a gain, the
raw vertical passes through a causal chain: a two-pole high-pass at 0.075~Hz,
integration and the same high-pass. Pd($\tau$) is the largest absolute displacement
in the $\tau$~s after the station's onset, for $\tau$ of 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7 and 10~s,
with the noise level taken over the 10~s ending 1~s before it. The relation
between $\log_{10}$Pd, magnitude and hypocentral distance has two magnitude slopes
and three distance slopes per window and a term per station. An event's magnitude is
the posterior mean of this relation's likelihood over its stations' values under a
Gutenberg--Richter prior; a value below three times its noise level enters as an
upper bound. The relation was fitted on 96\,908 values from 11\,806 KO events in a separate
station-disjoint data set; on its test stations, from M3 upward, the estimate at
$\tau = 5$~s has a bias within $\pm 0.05$ and a mean absolute error of 0.12 to 0.21.
With each estimate the pipeline reports the posterior probability of three size
classes, below M4, M4 to 5.5, and above; for events larger than about M6.5 the
estimate and its class are lower bounds. On KO
the calibration's gains, station terms and noise levels apply, with its exclusion of
channels whose noise lies far from their median. On Greece and New Zealand the gains
come from each set's StationXML, and a vertical channel is accepted only if its
response divided by the stated sensitivity lies within 0.8--1.25 between 0.1 and
5~Hz. On these two networks the station terms are set to zero and no noise levels
apply, so that no channel is excluded for being dead or in a coda. KO's station terms average $+0.07$ to
$+0.10$ in $\log_{10}$Pd, so zero terms bias an average station by about $+0.1$,
against the relation's scatter of about 0.3.

Pd requires hypocentral distances before the picks of the 60~s picker window can
locate an event. Each declared event is therefore also located from its detections'
onsets alone, by a grid search in a uniform half-space at 10~km depth from three
stations on, the onsets coming from a FilterPicker that runs beside the learned
detector and triggers nothing (median onset error 0.06~s on 418 development arrivals).
This location serves the Pd distances only, once its 68\% radius is below 20~km, until
a pick location replaces it (Section~S16). None of this touches detection,
association or matching: all development replays give the same alarms with and
without it.

\subsection{Scoring}\label{sec:protocols}

Alarms without a catalog counterpart are called unmatched rather than false, because
the catalogs are incomplete for small events during aftershock sequences; the
false-alarm rate is measured on the quiet windows, as alarms per day with exact
Poisson intervals. A declared event matches a catalog event if its epicenter lies
within 30~km and its origin within 5~s, or if the predicted P arrival at a station that raised the alarm
lies within 6~s of that station's P time, P being predicted in a uniform half-space.
Each alarm is credited to the one catalog event it fits best, and each catalog event
to the first alarm credited with it, so that no alarm counts for two events and a
second alarm for the same event counts as unmatched. The chance level $c$ is the
detection rate with all catalog times shifted by $\pm 127$, $\pm 263$ and $\pm 391$~s,
as the time slides of gravitational-wave analysis measure accidental coincidences
\citep{was2010},
and with observed rate $o$ the chance-corrected rate is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chance}
  \hat{o} = (o - c)/(1 - c),
\end{equation}
reported as an equivalent number of events; inside aftershock sequences most shifted
events land within 60~s of a real one, so $c$ overstates chance matching and the
correction is conservative (Section~S17). Window probabilities are cached, which
reproduces full inference exactly. Two detectors are compared on per-event outcomes
by an exact McNemar test and a paired bootstrap of the difference in chance-corrected
detections (10\,000 resamples within each sequence).

\subsection{Test protocol}\label{sec:protocol}

The protocol was committed to the pipeline's public repository and timestamped on
Zenodo before any test waveform was fetched (release v1.0.0, commit 3e43064,
2026-10-30, doi:10.5281/zenodo.23806743), with the release that is tested, the
hashes of each model file and of the static binary, and the code that fetches,
replays and scores the data. It is summarized here and given in full in Section~S24.

\paragraph{Detectors.} The learned detector keeps the rule fixed on the tuning data.
All baselines, \stalta{}, FilterPicker and the published PhaseNet and EQTransformer
models, were retuned on 29~October 2026 on all development event replays, the four
AFAD sequences and the KOERI \Mw{}~4.9 window, 197 cataloged events, by three
rules. The first takes the most chance-corrected detections at no more
unmatched alarms than the learned detector, the second the fewest unmatched alarms at
no fewer chance-corrected detections, and the third the most chance-corrected
detections at no more alarms than the learned detector on the AFAD quiet windows.
Because some unmatched alarms in aftershock sequences are uncataloged earthquakes,
more of them for a sensitive detector, the third rule matches the rate on data without
cataloged seismicity instead. For the published models the third rule is applied
within the weights the first two select. A per-station \stalta{} stands for the
per-station tuning of operational practice: the first rule's setting with each
station's own trigger ratio, the lowest of eight values at which the station's trigger
rate on four 6~h windows from before the test period does not exceed its network's
median rate at ratio~8 (Section~S24). Table~\ref{tab:settings} lists all settings.
The pipeline's transformer detector is fixed in the same release and tested under the
same protocol at its defaults; it is reported in a companion paper.

\paragraph{Endpoints.} The primary endpoint, on each network, is (i) the difference in
chance-corrected detections between the learned detector and \stalta{} at equal
unmatched alarms over its sequences, with a 95\% paired bootstrap interval resampled
within each sequence. The secondary endpoints are (ii) the same difference against
\stalta{} at equal quiet alarms and (iii) against each other detector; (iv)
equivalence with causal PhaseNet by two one-sided tests at 5\% with a margin of 10\%
of the scorable events; (v) each detector's alarm rate on the quiet windows, also
without the network's two stations of most \stalta{} detections; (vi) the rule's
adjacent settings (thresholds 0.75 and 0.85, 6 and 10 windows); (vii) the share of
alarm detections declared before the predicted S, and the primary comparison on the
events detected before S; (viii) on KO, the primary comparison on test-split stations
apart from training-split ones; (ix) the magnitude's error, bias and class accuracy
by band and the share of alarmed events with an estimate, for events no deeper than
40~km; and (x) alarm delay and warning time before S. Because the matcher predicts P
and S in a uniform half-space, 3 to 12~s late for events deeper than 100~km, each
detection endpoint is also reported for the events no deeper than 40~km. No
multiplicity correction is applied, each secondary result is labeled as such, and
nothing is retuned on test data. The stopping rule counts events from the catalog
before any waveform is fetched; the endpoints use the events ayzek scores (those with
  a depth and a magnitude, with origin from 30~s after the replay's start to 30~s before
its end), a few fewer per sequence.

\paragraph{Prospective tests.} The same protocol is applied to New Zealand and KO
data recorded after the release date, data that did not exist when the method was
fixed: all qualifying sequences from that date on, until the target or 12 months,
each fetched once when four weeks old, with 24 quiet windows drawn once the span has
closed (Section~S24). The archive delivers these data intact, so the test replays
them and is not a live run.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

We argue that a detector for early warning should be evaluated at the operating
point at which it will be used, on data it has not seen, and under a comparison fixed
before those data were chosen, and this study was designed accordingly; at one
development station, event-level ROC-AUC barely separated two detectors that the
operating point separated clearly (Section~S14). The held-out tests answer the question posed in the
Introduction. On New Zealand, a network the learned detector had not seen, the advantage held: 18
more chance-corrected events than \stalta{} at equal unmatched alarms, out of 151,
with an interval well clear of zero, and the same within 2.5 events for the shallow
events alone and for the events detected before S. On Greece the difference was
larger, 22 events out of 177, and the same for the shallow events, but it came with
twice \stalta{}'s unmatched alarms, so the comparison was not at an equal operating
point there.
On KO, with 36 events, the difference had the same sign and an interval including
zero, as the protocol anticipated; it is evidence of consistency, not a test.

On these data the classical detectors are neither obsolete nor superior. On
development data the learned detector gained in M2--4 events that an amplitude criterion cannot
separate from noise, and it kept that sensitivity at a quarter of the quiet-data
alarm rate of \stalta{} at rule 1 (3.74 against 14.7 per day), though \stalta{} at
rule 3 was quieter still. On New Zealand its rate stayed low,
0.9 alarms per day, but at 5 alarms against \stalta{}'s 9 not distinguishably lower.
On Greece and KO it did not stay low: 19.3 and 9.7 per day, while the
classical baselines matched on quiet alarms stayed near their development rates. An analysis
outside the protocol, made after the Greek result, separates the causes
(Section~S25). In the two Greek sequences with the most unmatched alarms, the learned
detector's 225 divide into 77 that locate within 30~km of the mainshock, 7 that follow
within 60~s events missing from NOA's catalog, 135 that could not be located and 6
located elsewhere. Causal PhaseNet, at the same development operating point and with
equivalent detections, raised 115 there, 53 located in the sequences: of the learned
detector's excess, 24 locate in the sequences and 84 cannot be located, at Evia
mostly with one station. On the quiet windows, one 6~h window in Crete holds 51 of the learned
detector's 106 Greek alarms, nearly all with station STIA, on emergent signals that no
catalog explains; \stalta{} did not fire
on them, so the protocol's exclusion of the two stations noisiest for \stalta{} did
not remove them. Without that window the learned detector raised 10.4 alarms per day
against 21.2, with that exclusion 12.2 against 5.8. The learned detector thus finds a
few more small uncataloged earthquakes than the other detectors, but most of its
excess alarms on Greece are station responses that \stalta{} and PhaseNet do not
share, and an unmatched or quiet-window alarm count does not separate the two.
\stalta{} alarmed earlier at the median on every network, by 1.2~s on Greece, 1.8~s
on New Zealand and 2.9~s on KO, since a window detector can trigger only once the P
wave occupies part of its 6~s window; for early warning this is a real cost. Classical
triggers also cost orders of magnitude less and time the onset to a sample, which is
what the pipeline uses them for. Causal PhaseNet, trained on Italian data, was
equivalent to the learned detector on both unseen networks, with fewer alarms on
Greece.
The gain over \stalta{} is therefore that of learned detectors generally, not of this
one; what this study adds is a pipeline that runs such a detector, with 142\,059
parameters per ensemble member and no machine-learning runtime, and a protocol that
measures it.

Much of the protocol rests on the computational design: cached window
probabilities and deterministic replays made trigger grids of hundreds of settings
and the retuning of all baselines practical, and the release, the request records
and the file hashes let anyone fetch the same test data again and check them. Only
complete replays exposed two failures of the data path, among them an earlier version
that dropped samples under load and lost 15 of 44 detections (Section~S2).

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

The KO test is far under its target, with
36 events in two sequences and 14 quiet windows, so its interval is wide; the
prospective KO test will add to it. The catalogs are incomplete for
small events, so that unmatched alarms only bound false alarms, and EMSC's catalog
lists fewer small events in KO's region than AFAD's does, which lowers KO's scorable
counts and raises all detectors' unmatched alarms there. The matcher predicts P and
S in a uniform half-space, so events deeper than about 150~km are scored as missed
for all detectors alike, which the depth-restricted results address.
Parameters shared by all detectors, such as the 40~s coda absorption and the 15~s
between a station's triggers, were not swept. The learned detector responds to emergent station noise that \stalta{} ignores
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}), which the protocol's station exclusion, keyed to
\stalta{}, does not catch. The ranking on a continuous record rests on one station,
and the published models ran with pre-trained weights that fine-tuning
might improve. The input is a replay of archived files; a live SeedLink client, with
its latency, packet loss and clock problems, was not part of the tests.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}

A self-contained, verified real-time pipeline and a replay protocol with chance
correction, paired tests and catalog-quiet data measure seismic detectors at their operating
point. Under this protocol, on the Greek and New Zealand networks that the learned
detector had not seen, it found 22.4 (Greece) and 18.1 (New Zealand) more
chance-corrected events than \stalta{} at the settings matched on development data,
with 95\% intervals 11.7--33.5 and 7.2--29.5. On Greece this came at twice
\stalta{}'s unmatched alarms, on both networks at quiet-data alarm rates not
distinguishable from \stalta{}'s, though on Greece the comparison turns on which
stations are included, and with alarms 1.2 to 1.8~s later; on KO, held out in
time and under its target, the difference was 2.5, with an interval including zero.
Causal PhaseNet was equivalent to the learned detector on both unseen networks, so the
gain is that of learned detectors over an amplitude trigger. At one development
station, two detectors with nearly equal event AUC differed by a factor of 1.6 in
recall at one window false alarm per day. Our results indicate that detectors for early warning
are better compared on continuous records at fixed alarm budgets.
