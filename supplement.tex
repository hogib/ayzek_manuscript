\section{Verification and the data path}\label{s:verification}

\paragraph{Numerical agreement.} Every network of the pipeline is a
transcription of its PyTorch module and reads the same \texttt{state\_dict}.
The test suite (15 tests, \tool{meson test}) compares each stage with its
reference on real waveforms (Table~\ref{t:verification}a). Window conditioning
runs in double precision as SciPy does; the residual of the cleaned signal is
the rounding of an eighth-order IIR filter in transfer-function form. The
picker's picks are identical to PyTorch's. The aarch64 build passes the same
suite under emulation and, on a Raspberry~Pi~4, raises the same alarms at the
same times with the same locations as the x86 build on the KOERI datasets.

\paragraph{Undecodable records.} During the \Mw{}~6.2 of 2025-04-23, the
dataloggers at CATL (57~km) and ARNA (68~km) switched from Steim-2 compression
to uncompressed 32-bit integer records (CATL little-endian, ARNA big-endian);
ELBA, at 38~km, did not. A decoder that accepted only Steim-2 rejected all of them, 104 records at CATL and 155 at ARNA, at 09:49--09:51 and 10:02--10:03 UTC, and the two stations therefore recorded no detection of the strongest shaking. The
pipeline now decodes integer records in either byte order (both files decode
bit-exactly against ObsPy) and reports, with the reason, any record it cannot
decode. A scan of the whole archive (its 13 stations, the eleven of the continuous
archive and ARNA and CATL; 283 chunks, 398 million records, 2024--2026) found 304 non-Steim-2 records, all at ARNA and CATL on
that day between 09:49 and 12:13 UTC.

\paragraph{Sample loss under load.} The pipeline dates a sample by its position
in a ring of $2^{17}$ samples (21.8~min at 100~Hz) per component. An earlier
implementation dropped the samples it could not hold when a ring was full. A
network-wide archive outage of 2{,}095.6--2{,}103.4~s per component (1.60 times the ring, on all 27 components of nine stations at once) exposed the defect. Because position is ring index plus base, a dropped sample does not leave a hole but moves every later sample of that component earlier, and after the outage the three components of a station no longer describe the same instant.
In the current pipeline, writes never wait and never drop. What does not fit is queued per component, and a gap is queued as a count, so an outage of any length costs no memory. Table~\ref{t:verification}b repeats the Marmara replay
with a 25-minute hole cut into the record of all eight stations, ending 49~min
before the \Mw{}~6.2. With the current pipeline the hole changes nothing but
the two single-station detections inside it; with the earlier one, 329{,}439
dropped samples cost 15 of the 44 detections and the \Mw{}~5.9. The 11.6-hour
window containing the outage is replayed without a stall or a drop, and
yields exactly the windows, triggers, picks and alarms of the two runs split
around it (711{,}760 windows, 86 triggers and the same five alarms to the
hundredth of a second); only the windows rejected for crossing the gap differ.

\section{Station subsets and alert time}\label{s:subsets}

The Marmara replay was recorded once (\tool{ayzek --record}) and its network
stage rerun for all 247 subsets of two to eight of its stations
(\tool{tools/network_subsets}), which gives the same result as replaying each
subset. Every subset declares the \Mw{}~6.2, and the best subset of every size
alerts at 11.0~s. Table~\ref{t:subsets}a gives the best subset per size and
Table~\ref{t:subsets}b the mean change when a station is added to a subset
without it. The four stations within 70~km each shorten the \Mw{}~6.2 alert by
about 7~s; SEMS and CATL add the most declared events and also the most
declared events without a catalog counterpart.

Against a geometric model in which the alert is the P travel time to the
subset's second-nearest station plus a latency, the observed alerts of the 247
subsets give a latency of median 2.05~s (interquartile range 0.58--2.77~s) and
a rank correlation of 0.71 between predicted and observed alert. The largest
residuals, 5.7~s, are the subsets in which KIRK (140~km) is the second station, because KIRK stays below the 0.9 plateau for the \Mw{}~6.2 and declares through the persistence branch.

\section{Association slack}\label{s:slack}

Two detections may belong to one event if their P times differ by no more than
the P travel time between the stations plus a slack, 3~s by default. The slack
was sized for the P estimate before anchoring, good to about 2.5~s; anchored P
times are good to about 0.1~s, so a tighter slack might be expected to reject
chance pairings. It does not. No count changes by more than one event or one alarm (Table~\ref{t:slack}), because the admissible difference is dominated by
the inter-station travel time, 7~s for ARNA--ELBA and about 50~s for the
widest pairs of these networks.

\section{Three-station declaration}\label{s:minstations}

Requiring a third station halves the unmatched alarms of the dense Marmara
network for 1.2~s of median delay and 0.5~s on the \Mw{}~6.2. In the sparse
S\i{}nd\i{}rg\i{} \Mw{}~6.1 network the third station is 230--290~km away, and the \Mw{}~6.1 alarm

\section{Published models: adapter, selection and details}\label{s:seisbench}

\paragraph{Adapter.} \tool{tools/seisbench_scores.py} (SeisBench 0.12.6)
writes, for every 6~s window of the pipeline's 0.5~s grid, the largest model
output within the window, which is the detection trace of EQTransformer or the P
probability of PhaseNet. In the causal mode the model input ends at the window's
end (60~s for EQTransformer, 30.01~s for PhaseNet); in the look-ahead mode it is
centered on the window. Each model applies its own normalization;
EQTransformer's original weights carry a 1--45~Hz band-pass, applied causally in
the causal mode and zero-phase in the look-ahead mode. The scores replace the
detector through \tool{--scores-in}; everything downstream is the pipeline's.
Every replay was checked to take every window from these scores (detector time
0.001--0.002~ms per window on every station).

\paragraph{Selection.} The protocol, the candidate weights and the two rules
were committed before the first tuning run (91b9ee1 of the paper's repository,
22 October 2026; the one adapter check that preceded it is recorded in 571ef5f). The candidates were the original, STEAD, INSTANCE and ETHZ weights of EQTransformer and the STEAD, INSTANCE and ETHZ weights of PhaseNet. The trigger grid on the two tuning replays crossed thresholds of 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, \ldots, 0.9 with a persistence of 1, 2, 4 or 8 windows, with the instant branch off and the chance correction applied. Table~\ref{t:seisbench}a gives the selection. For the record,
the best setting of the other weight sets at most 26 unmatched alarms reached
15.9 (EQTransformer ETHZ), 3.5 (original) and none (STEAD) chance-corrected
detections in the causal mode, and PhaseNet ETHZ 45.4 and STEAD 31.4.
Table~\ref{t:seisbench}b and~c add, for the evaluation run once with these
settings, the S\i{}nd\i{}rg\i{} \Mw{}~6.1 magnitude bands and alarm delays and the KOERI
datasets one by one; marmara\_ko overlaps the detector's training data (134 windows
at four stations) and demonstrates the pipeline without comparing detectors.

\section{The quiet-window alarms, one by one}\label{s:waveforms}

Table~\ref{t:alarms} lists the 16 alarms of the learned detector on the AFAD
quiet windows with the class of Table~\ref{t:devresults}b. The classes use two criteria
that need no judgment of the waveforms. \emph{KIRK pulse}: KIRK declared the
alarm and its detection fell in the slot of its periodic pulse, 50--60~s after a
10-minute mark (218 of its 225 detections fall there, Figure~\ref{f:quietexamples}c; in
these nine alarms at 58.6--58.8~s); the alarm itself follows when the partner
station's detection completes the pair, 62--105~s after the mark.
\emph{Seismic, picker-confirmed}: the rule below confirms a seismic source in at
least one of its four runs. \emph{Undetermined}: neither applies. For alarm~1 the
pipeline's own picks suggest that an earthquake at BAND and CMH coincided with
KIRK's pulse; no published picker confirms it, and the alarm is counted as a
pulse.

The Pickers column applies one rule, fixed before it was run, to every alarm of both detectors. A published picker must find P and S at the first two declaring
stations at a probability of at least 0.5, each S$-$P interval gives a distance (\VP{}~=~6.0, \VS{}~=~3.5~km/s), and the two distances must fit the station
separation and predict the observed difference of the two P picks to within
1.5~s. The four codes are PhaseNet with ETHZ and with STEAD weights, then
EQTransformer with ETHZ and with STEAD weights (SeisBench 0.12.6; the INSTANCE
weights, which are compared as detectors in the main text, are not used):
C, confirmed; I, picks at both stations that fail the test; 1, a P and S pair at
one station only; 0, at neither. No run confirms an alarm of KIRK's pulse. The
same rule confirms three of the 323 \stalta{} alarms, the events of alarms 13,
14 and 15.

The figures show every station pair that detected within the coincidence window
of one of the alarms and has the pipeline's P and S picks at both stations (19
pairs; alarm~11 has none), titled by the alarm and its class; the picks are shown
for orientation and the time in each title is the P pick at the first station,
not an origin time. After them comes a random sample (seed 20261022) of 30 of the
323 \stalta{} alarms, of which 19 have P and S picks to mark. Band-pass
1--20~Hz; P picks solid, S picks dashed; the three components of a panel share one
amplitude scale. The distances in the titles follow from the S$-$P intervals of
the picks whether or not the picks lie on seismic arrivals; for an alarm without a
seismic source they have no physical meaning.

\paragraph{Sensitivity analyses.} Two analyses, specified before they were run
(Table~S13), test how conservative the classification is. Applied to
every pair of stations associated with an alarm (the declaring stations and any
  station whose detection is compatible with theirs under the pipeline's association
rule) rather than to the first two declaring stations, the rule also confirms alarm~1 in three of its four runs, through the BAND--CMH pair, as a local event that coincided with KIRK's pulse. It confirms no other pulse alarm and leaves alarms 4,
8 and 16 undetermined. For \stalta{} it confirms six alarms, two of them on events
the learned detector did not alarm on. Without the confirmed alarms the rates are
2.57 and 74.0 per day. KOERI's FDSN service has no event service, so the EMSC
catalog and the ISC bulletin, which carries KOERI's own locations, were searched for every alarm (origin up to 180~s before it, within 300~km). The only events that fit an alarm are the M2.4 of alarm~2 (EMSC and KOERI) and an M1.9 located by KOERI for alarm~13. Neither catalog lists any event for the undetermined alarms 4, 8 and 16, or for any pulse alarm. KIRK's pulse does not appear in the event replays. One of 38 KIRK detections on the Marmara replay falls in the pulse slot, in no unmatched alarm, and none of 20 on the \Mw{}~6.1 replay, and every pulse alarm on the quiet windows falls between 23 May and 9 June 2024.

\section{Detector ranking on a continuous record}\label{s:swave}

Six detectors were scored on a continuous 728-day record at the development station
MANT: the learned detector and the pipeline's \stalta{}, and four offline detectors,
a classical \stalta{} (Allen, 1978), two P-wave detectors that score 3.4~s windows
and differ only in their noise windows, and a 6~s detector with station scaling
(Table~\ref{t:swave}a, Figure~\ref{f:recall}). Windows from 10~s before to 60~s after
the P arrival of any cataloged event within 500~km form its guard and the rest the
background; the threshold for a budget of $B$ window false alarms per day is the
score that $B$ background windows a day exceed, and an event is detected when the
highest score in its guard exceeds it. The pipeline's two detectors are almost
indistinguishable by event AUC (0.9910 for \stalta{}, 0.9892 for the learned
detector; paired difference on the common events 0.0016, 0.0000--0.0035), yet at one
window false alarm per day the learned detector finds 34.8\% of the events against
22.2\%, 1.6 times as many, and among the offline detectors recall at that budget is in
the exact reverse order of event AUC. The inversion is a property of the metric,
which integrates over all false positive rates whereas operation uses rates of order
$10^{-4}$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/fig5_recall_budget.pdf}
  \caption{Recall of the six detectors of Table~\ref{t:swave}a against the alarm budget
    (window false alarms per day), 20 budgets a decade; dots mark the budgets of the
    table. Solid wide lines: detectors run by the pipeline; dashed: their offline
    counterparts.}\label{f:recall}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Without the S wave.} Panel (a) scores an event by the highest window score in its guard, from 10~s before the predicted P (minus the window length) to 60~s after it, and every detector can therefore score the S wave and coda as well as the P wave. Here an event
is scored only from windows that end at least a margin before its predicted S
arrival (IASP91), and only events whose S$-$P interval is at least 6~s plus the
margin are counted, so that every detector, including the 6~s detectors on their
6~s grid, has a window ending between P and that limit. Thresholds are those of panel (a), because they are set by the background, which does not depend on how events are scored. The event AUC compares each event's maximum with the maximum over one
random stretch of record of the same length, clear of every cataloged event.

\section{Selection sensitivity, match criteria, public event sets and location}\label{s:more}

\paragraph{The equal-unmatched rule.} The rule was applied to the 25 unmatched
alarms that the learned detector had on the tuning data when it was fixed. The
anchoring of P times, introduced later, moved the learned detector to 48.7
chance-corrected detections and 26 unmatched alarms without changing either
selection; applied to 26, the rule would select another \stalta{} setting
(1--10~Hz, STA 0.5~s, LTA 10~s, ratio 8), with 44.8 chance-corrected detections and
26 unmatched alarms on the tuning data, 26.7 with 3 on the \Mw{}~6.1 data and 14.7
alarms per day on the quiet windows (the open diamond of Figure~\ref{f:curves}). The
learned detector remains ahead on the \Mw{}~6.1 data by about ten events rather
than 15, against a \stalta{} with fewer unmatched alarms.

\paragraph{Anchoring.} Anchoring changes only the time attached to a detection,
but association and catalog matching use that time, and it can therefore change which detections form an alarm and which catalog event an alarm is matched to. On the tuning replays it moved the adopted rule from 50.0 to 48.7 chance-corrected
detections and from 25 to 26 unmatched alarms. Moving the picker window to the
anchored P time as well increased the median epicenter error on the Marmara replay
from 9.4 to 10.3~km, and the picker therefore keeps the detector's clock.

\paragraph{Match criteria and close events.} Replayed without the picker, and so
without locations, the learned detector keeps every catalog match on all four
event replays, and \stalta{} loses 2 of its 30 matches on Marmara; matches rest on
the P-time criterion. Because later detections within 40~s at a station are
absorbed, an event that closely follows another is harder to detect. Of the 188 catalog events, 24 of the event replays follow the previous one within 40~s, and of these the learned detector detects 9 and the two \stalta{} settings 7 and 14.

\paragraph{The public KOERI event sets.} On the S\i{}nd\i{}rg\i{} \Mw{}~4.9 window,

\section{The chance level of each detector}\label{s:chance}

The chance level $c$ of main-text Equation~2 is the share of catalog events
matched when every catalog time is shifted by $\pm 127$, $\pm 263$ or $\pm 391$~s.
Inside an aftershock sequence most shifted events land within 60~s of a real one
(52 of 69 per shift on the \Mw{}~6.1 replay, 48 of 62 on Marmara), where a true
detection of the real event can match them; $c$ then measures chance matching where
alarms are densest. Table~\ref{t:chance} gives $c$ for every real-time detector on
every event replay, as used and with the shifted events that land within 60~s of a
real one removed. Without them $c$ falls for every detector; it falls most for the
detectors with the most true detections, and the chance-corrected counts rise by
1.5 to 3.5 events. The paired differences between detectors move by at most 0.8
events (\Mw{}~6.1 against \stalta{} at equal unmatched alarms, 14.7, then 14.6 with 30~s and 14.6 with 60~s removed), and the correction is therefore conservative for every
detector and favors none.

\section{Training data, phase picker and offline detectors}\label{s:training}

\paragraph{Training data.} The detection dataset consists of 6~s three-component
windows from 181 stations of the KO, 6G and IJ networks (156, 17 and 8 stations),
retrieved from the KOERI FDSN service for every station within 0.5\textdegree{} of
cataloged events of $M \geq 2$. Earthquake windows start 2~s before the IASP91 P
arrival from the catalog hypocenter, and noise windows are cut from 300~s records
beginning 3~h before each event, with the two classes balanced 1:1 and the noise
windows selected so that their amplitude distribution, measured as the raw-count
standard deviation of the loudest component, matches that of the earthquake windows.
The dataset was partitioned by station, and all windows of a station, of either
class, belong to one of the training, validation and test sets. The one exception is
two earthquake windows of the training stations KRBG and MDNY, whose network code was
missing in the source records and which were assigned to the validation and test
sets, a change of at most $10^{-4}$ in the test ROC-AUC. Because about 97\% of the
stations contribute windows of both classes, allocating the classes independently
would have placed nearly every station in training under one label and in evaluation
under the other. No station of the dataset belongs to the TU archive. Of the 15
stations of the public KOERI replays, 9 belong to the training set, 2 to the
validation set and 4 to the test set (Table~S1a). The detector was trained with a
dropout rate of 0.4 and early stopping, and the three seeds' probabilities are
averaged.

\paragraph{Phase picker.} Location relies on the waveform arm of a P/S picker whose
dual-channel design follows Wang and Zhao (2025); in an ablation its second,
image-based arm added nothing to pick accuracy. The waveform arm comprises three
strided convolutions (kernels of 9, 9 and 7 samples, each followed by batch
normalization and ReLU), average pooling to 250 segments of 0.24~s, a bidirectional
LSTM, four-head self-attention and a per-segment two-layer classifier over the classes
noise, P and S (94\,659 parameters). It was trained with soft, PhaseNet-style targets
on 30\,000 earthquake traces from chunk~2 of STEAD for four epochs with a single seed,
using a station-disjoint split of 137, 33 and 32 receivers; on the held-out receivers
the median absolute deviation of its S picks is 0.07~s. In the pipeline each 60~s
window is detrended, tapered, band-passed and standardized per component, without
the asinh transform, and the P and S picks are the segments of highest P and S
probability. Because the training data contain no noise traces, the picker returns
picks with probabilities of about 0.7 even for pure noise. It is therefore run only
after the detector has triggered, and a pick is accepted only if both probabilities
are at least 0.5, S follows P by 0--60~s, and P lies between 3~s before and 9~s after
the start of the triggering window. Location is a two-stage grid search ($\pm 3^\circ$
at $0.05^\circ$, then $\pm 0.1^\circ$ at $0.005^\circ$) at 10~km depth in a half-space
(\VP{}~=~6.0, \VS{}~=~3.5~km/s), the worst station being removed while the RMS residual
exceeds 2~s.

\paragraph{Offline detectors and station scaling.} The offline networks of the
continuous-record ranking normalize each channel by the long-term mean and standard
deviation of its station, accumulated over six archive chunks spread evenly across the
record and cleaned in 1~h pieces with the detrending, taper and band-pass applied to
the windows. The two P-wave detectors were trained on a separate station-disjoint
KOERI dataset of 111\,190 windows (120, 28 and 35 stations) that extend from 2.0~s
before to 1.4~s after the predicted P arrival, and differ only in the selection of
their noise windows. The offline 6~s detector has the corpus and split of the
pipeline's detector with station scaling in place of per-window standardization
(curated test ROC-AUC 0.9896). The pipeline's per-window detector, scored on the same
disjoint windows, reproduces the pipeline's scores to a median of $2\times10^{-6}$
(maximum $1.3\times10^{-4}$ over 3\,702 windows).

\section{Development results in full}\label{s:devresults}

Before the release, the two AFAD sequences of main-text Table~1 (S\i{}nd\i{}rg\i{}
\Mw{}~6.1 and Simav \Mw{}~4.8) and the 23 quiet windows served as this study's test
bed, and the settings in use then were the ones selected on the two tuning windows
alone. Those results fixed the method, from the trigger rule to the third selection
rule of the retuning, and the test protocol counts them as development data. They are
given here as they stood, at the pre-retuning settings; the retuned settings of
main-text Table~3 replaced them before the tests.

\paragraph{Trigger rule.} Relative to the single window at $p \geq 0.9$, the adopted
rule detects 39 instead of 24 of the 66 \Mw{}~6.1 events, at 7 instead of 2 unmatched
alarms (Section~\ref{s:grid}). The gain lies in M2--4 events, and large events, which
saturate the detector and fire the instant branch, keep their alarm times (\Mw{}~6.2 at
+11.0~s, \Mw{}~6.1 at +17.0~s and \Mw{}~4.9 at +18.0~s after origin). On the \Mw{}~6.1
replay 8, 10 and 12 windows detect 39, 29 and 25 events, so that the adopted setting
lies at the edge of a steep decline. Over the full grids (Figure~\ref{f:curves}a), the
learned detector's envelope lies above that of \stalta{} at every count of unmatched
alarms from 1 upward except 14, where both reach 38.6. A single threshold just below the
plateau ($p \geq 0.895$) performs comparably but is as sensitive to the plateau's
height (Section~\ref{s:singlewindow}).

\paragraph{Learned detector versus \stalta{}.} \stalta{} settings were tuned over 648
combinations, and two rules fixed before the run selected one setting at equal
unmatched alarms (2--20~Hz, 1 and 30~s, ratio 8, vertical) and one at equal detections
(2--10~Hz, 0.5 and 10~s, ratio 6, vertical). At comparable unmatched alarms (7 and 9),
the learned detector detects 68\% more \Mw{}~6.1 events, with 36.4 against 21.7
chance-corrected detections, a difference of 14.7 (95\% interval 5.9--23.7; 7.9--22.2
with 10-minute blocks; exact McNemar $p = 0.003$). The difference lies in M2--4 events
(12 against 4 of 27 M2--3 events and 21 against 14 of 32 M3--4 events), whereas
\stalta{} detects all six M4--5 events and the learned detector five. At comparable
detections (37.9 against 36.4), \stalta{} raises about three times the unmatched
alarms, 26 against 7 on the \Mw{}~6.1 replay and 63 against 26 on the tuning data
(Table~\ref{t:devresults}a, Section~\ref{s:grid}). \stalta{} alarms 1.0--1.3~s earlier
on the two largest events, because it triggers on the onset sample whereas the window
detector must see the P wave inside a 6~s window.

\paragraph{Other detectors and the Simav sequence.} For each published model and for
FilterPicker, the weights and trigger setting were selected on the tuning replays by
the two rules above, and both rules chose the INSTANCE weights for the published
models. The Simav sequence was replayed once with every setting fixed, on the six
archive stations within 300~km, against 56 cataloged events, 39 of them below M2
(Table~\ref{t:devresults}a, Figure~\ref{f:detectors}). On Simav the direction of the
\stalta{} comparison repeats, but not its size: at comparable unmatched alarms (17 and
14) the learned detector detected 35.1 chance-corrected events against 29.1, a
difference of 6.0 whose interval includes zero, and the gain again lies in M2--4
events. Most of Simav's events are below M2, the lower limit of the detector's training
events. Pooled over both sequences, with the chance correction and bootstrap within
each, the difference is 20.7 (8.8--32.8; exact McNemar $p = 0.001$), a summary that
neither protocol specified. The gain over \stalta{} is not specific to the pipeline's
detector: causal PhaseNet matched it on both sequences (pooled difference 1.4, $-8.1$
to 11.0), with 12 and 28 unmatched alarms against 7 and 17 and with 15 quiet-window
alarms against 16, whereas causal EQTransformer was not competitive at equal unmatched
alarms. With look-ahead, PhaseNet detected the most events of any detector, but only
offline and at three times the unmatched alarms and twice the quiet-window rate of the
learned detector (7.2 against 3.74 per day, exact $p = 0.04$); ending the input 1 or
2~s after the window left PhaseNet level with the learned detector and EQTransformer far
below it (Section~\ref{s:ahead}). FilterPicker is the strongest classical detector
tested: at equal unmatched alarms it detected fewer \Mw{}~6.1 events than the learned
detector (difference 7.6, 1.4 to 14.1; McNemar $p = 0.021$) and as many on Simav, and
at equal detections it matched the learned detector with two to three times its
unmatched alarms. On the quiet windows its two settings raised 82.4 and 207.1 alarms
per day, 22 and 55 times the learned detector's rate, mostly at two stations, CMH and
KIRK.

\paragraph{False alarms on quiet data.} Over 102.8~h without a cataloged event within
250~km, the learned detector raised 16 alarms, 3.74 per day (95\% interval 2.14--6.07),
against 323, 75.4 per day (67.4--84.1), for \stalta{} at equal unmatched alarms (rate
ratio 0.050, 0.028--0.082). That ratio of about 20 belongs to the two selected
settings, because \stalta{} was matched on unmatched rather than false alarms; the
third selection rule of the retuning answers it. Over the full grids
(Figure~\ref{f:curves}b), at equal tuning detections, the quietest \stalta{} setting
raises 5 to 29 times the alarms of the quietest learned setting (4.9 against 0.93 per
day at 30 chance-corrected detections, 14.7 against 1.63 at 44 and 60.5 against 2.1 at
48.7). The rate of \stalta{} is similar in the \Mw{}~6.1 window (72 per day) and on
quiet data, whereas the learned detector's falls from 56 to 3.74 per day, which
suggests that most of its unmatched alarms in the event windows were uncataloged
aftershocks. On 5.8~h of public KOERI quiet data the learned detector raised 1 alarm
against 25 for \stalta{}. The 16 alarms were classified by criteria that need no
judgment of the waveforms (Table~\ref{t:devresults}b, Section~\ref{s:waveforms}). Nine
were declared with KIRK, whose detection fell 58.6--58.8~s after a 10-minute mark, the
slot of a periodic instrumental pulse, a single 0.3~s pulse locked to UTC
(Figure~\ref{f:quietexamples}), confined to May--June 2024 and absent from the event
replays; a faulty station raises the network's alarm rate through chance coincidences
with the other stations' triggers. A rule fixed before it was run confirms a seismic
source when a published picker finds P and S at two declaring stations and the two
S$-$P distances fit the station separation and the P-time difference; it confirms four
alarms of the learned detector, one an M2.4 listed by EMSC but not by AFAD, and three
of the 323 \stalta{} alarms, all among the same events. Without them the rates are 2.80
and 74.7 per day, and without KIRK the learned detector's rate would be 1.40 per day.
Of the 323 \stalta{} alarms, 263 rest on signals shorter than 3~s at both stations,
most of them spikes under 0.5~s.

\paragraph{Alert latency and station subsets.} For the Marmara \Mw{}~6.2, the alarm
came 11.0~s after origin, and the S wave reached station ELBA (38~km) 2.1~s later.
Over every subset of stations that declared the \Mw{}~6.2, the alert exceeds the P
travel time to the second-nearest station by a median of 2.05~s (interquartile range
0.6--2.8~s), and two stations alone, ARNA and ELBA, give the same alert as all eight
(Section~\ref{s:subsets}).

\section{The test protocol's rules in full, and the secondary endpoints}\label{s:protocolfull}

The protocol's text is \tool{docs/protocol-heldout.md} of release v1.0.0; this section
gives the rules the main text summarizes and the secondary endpoints in full.

\paragraph{Per-station \stalta{}.} The network-wide equal-unmatched setting
(main-text Table~3) with each station's trigger ratio replaced by the lowest of 3, 4,
5, 6, 8, 10, 12 and 15 at which the station's trigger rate on four 6~h windows from
before the test period (drawn with seed 20221231; Greece and New Zealand in 2022, KO
from 1~July to 29~August 2026) does not exceed its network group's target. The rate
counts only the time outside the arrivals of cataloged events the station could have
recorded; the target is the median station rate of the network-wide setting on the
same windows, 0.845, 0.548 and 0.318 triggers per quiet hour for Greece, KO and New
Zealand. A station above the target at every ratio gets 15, and a test station
without tuning data keeps ratio~8 (\tool{tools/stalta\_station\_ratios.py}). Before
the release the target was changed, on 29~October 2026, from the median station rate
on the AFAD quiet windows (0.307 per quiet hour, with the grid ending at 10), against
which most Greek and KO stations ended at the grid's top ratio.

\paragraph{Seeds and counts.} Reserve coin 42 (Python's \tool{random.Random(42)}, one
draw per sequence, below 0.5 is this study's); quiet windows 43, prospective quiet
windows 44, tuning windows 20221231. The stopping rule counts a sequence's scorable
events as the pool catalog's events with origin within the replay and epicenter
within 250~km of the stations' centroid, any magnitude, with or without depth; ayzek
scores the set's catalog excerpt, which leaves out events without depth or magnitude,
with origin from 30~s after the replay's start to 30~s before its end.

\paragraph{Deviations.} A bug found after the test data are fetched is fixed and the
results reported both ways, labeled as a deviation; nothing is retuned on test data.
One was found after the three runs, in the analysis tool and not in the pipeline.
Each report lists, for the stations of a detected event, the S arrival and the warning
before it, the S arrival picked where the pipeline's picker has a pick and predicted
otherwise. The tool of the release read only the predicted rows, so the warning-time
endpoint (x) left out the stations with a pick, those nearest the source, and on
Greece 23 of the learned detector's 153 detected events, every station of which had a
pick. The fix (commit 87e692c of the pipeline's repository) reads both kinds of row.
Rerun on the same reports, the analysis stage reproduces every other output file byte
for byte; Table~\ref{t:warning} gives the endpoint both ways. The one tooling fault, an out-of-date build of \tool{tools/network\_subsets} that stopped KO's analysis stage before it wrote any output, changed no number (Table~\ref{t:prereg}).

\paragraph{Prospective tests.} New Zealand and KO, on data recorded from the release
date on: every qualifying sequence in time order with no reserve split, until the
network's scorable events reach its target (150, 240) or 12 months have passed,
whichever comes first, in which case every sequence is used and the result is
reported as under target. Once a month, the qualifying sequences whose first event is
at least four weeks old are identified from the network's catalog and fetched with
the released tool, each once, with its catalog excerpt at that time; later catalog
revisions are not taken up. After the span closes, 24 quiet windows are drawn over it

\section{Exploratory: the Greek unmatched and quiet-window alarms}\label{s:exploratory}

This analysis is outside the protocol. It was made after the Greek result, when the
learned detector's 371 unmatched alarms (against 189 for \stalta{} at the setting
matched on the development data) and its 19.3 quiet-window alarms per day asked for an
explanation. Nothing in it changes a number of the protocol's endpoints.

\paragraph{Unmatched alarms in the two noisiest sequences.} An alarm counts as located
if the pipeline gave it a P-time location from at least five stations with a 68\%
radius of at most 20~km, or a pick location from at least three stations with an rms
of at most 1~s, and as located in the sequence if that location lies within 30~km of
the sequence's first event. At Epirus (MLh~4.6, 21~March 2023; station AMPL 5~km from
the epicenter, the other seven 41 to 52~km), 52 of the learned detector's 121
unmatched alarms locate in the sequence, 43 of them from P and S picks, 5 elsewhere
and 64 not at all; AMPL takes part in 77. An event located from eight stations at the
epicenter 8~min before the mainshock is absent from NOA's catalog. At Evia (MLh~5.1,
3~November 2023; all stations 51 to 77~km away), 25 of 104 locate in the sequence, 23
from picks, 2 elsewhere and 77 not at all, and the unmatched alarms per half hour rise
and decay as an aftershock sequence does (15, 21, 28, 17, 15, 8 from 10~min before the
mainshock). The ISC bulletin lists 9 events in the Evia window and 1 in the Epirus
window from the Aristotle University network that NOA's catalog lacks, and an
unmatched alarm of the learned detector follows each within 60~s. Four of the alarms
that follow them are among those located in the sequences, and 7 are not (6 unlocated,
1 located elsewhere). The 225 unmatched alarms therefore divide into 77 located in
the sequences, 7 others following an event missing from NOA's catalog, 135 other
unlocated alarms and 6 others located elsewhere; at least 84 (the first two groups)
are earthquakes missing from the catalog. The unlocated alarms, mostly declared by two
stations 40 to 80~km from the source, cannot be classified from the reports.

\paragraph{The same classification for the other detectors.} If the learned
detector's excess of unmatched alarms were mostly sensitivity to small earthquakes,
an equally sensitive detector would share it. Causal PhaseNet at rule 1 had the same
development operating point (52 unmatched alarms) and detected as many of the Greek
cataloged events (Table~5 of the main text). Table~\ref{t:control} classifies the
unmatched alarms of the three detectors in the two sequences by the same rule. Of the
learned detector's 110 unmatched alarms beyond causal PhaseNet's, 24 locate in the
sequences and 84 cannot be located; at Evia station VILL takes part in 50 of the
learned detector's unmatched alarms and in 5 of PhaseNet's, and in 38 of its 77
unlocated ones against 3 of PhaseNet's 20. Of the 9 Evia events
missing from NOA's catalog, the learned detector alarmed after 9, \stalta{} after 6 and
causal PhaseNet after 5. The learned detector thus finds a few more of the small
uncataloged events than the other two, but most of its excess over PhaseNet consists
of alarms that cannot be located and that PhaseNet, at equal detections of cataloged
events, does not raise.

\paragraph{The quiet windows.} Window 14 (central and eastern Crete, 19~November 2025,
03:20 to 09:20~UTC; stations CHAN, IPPL, KNDR, KNSS, PFKS, STIA, IMMV and SIVA) holds
51 of the learned detector's 106 Greek quiet alarms. STIA takes part in 49, KNSS in

EMSC nor the ISC lists an event within 1.5\textdegree{} in the window. \stalta{}
raised 2 alarms there. Without window 14 the learned detector raised 55 alarms in
126.5~h, 10.4 per day, against 112, 21.2 per day, for \stalta{} at equal unmatched
alarms. The protocol leaves out the two stations with the most \stalta{} detections,
CHAN and PFKS, which does not reach STIA because \stalta{} does not fire on its
signals.
